\section{Segmentation Backend and Configuration}
\label{sec:results_segmentation}

{\small The segmentation models used for object proposals (Section~\ref{subsec:method_proposals}) were evaluated offline on stored frames rather than inside the running pipeline, so that their masks could be measured against ground truth and their latency without interference from other components. The benchmark consists of 500 frames of the uHumans2 office scene~\cite{rosinol2021kimera} at $720 \times 480$ pixels, taken at intervals of about 0.75\,s of recording time. The simulator's segmentation image assigns every pixel to a class but does not separate individual objects, so each connected region of one class was treated as one ground-truth object. Regions smaller than 3500 pixels were excluded from the evaluation; a mask on them counts neither as correct nor as wrong. This yields 5231 ground-truth objects, on average 10.5 per frame; Figure~\ref{fig:results_gt_example} shows an example.\par}

{\small The masks were scored with the panoptic quality (PQ) of Kirillov et~al.~\cite{kirillov2019panoptic}, applied without class labels. A mask $p$ and a ground-truth object $g$ match if their intersection over union (IoU) exceeds 0.5; since neither masks nor objects overlap, each object has at most one such mask, and the result is independent of the order of the masks. Unmatched objects count as false negatives ($\mathit{FN}$), unmatched masks as false positives ($\mathit{FP}$). Pooled over all frames,
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{PQ} =
	\underbrace{\frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \mathit{TP}} \mathrm{IoU}(p,g)}{|\mathit{TP}|}}_{\text{segmentation quality (SQ)}}
	\times
	\underbrace{\frac{|\mathit{TP}|}{|\mathit{TP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathit{FP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathit{FN}|}}_{\text{recognition quality (RQ)}} ,
	\label{eq:pq}
\end{equation}
where $\mathit{TP}$ is the set of matched pairs. SQ measures how closely the found objects are outlined, RQ, an F1 score, how many objects are found without spurious masks. Since the score is class-agnostic and pooled, it is not comparable with published PQ results. All runs were performed on a Jetson AGX Thor (JetPack~7.2, TensorRT~10.16) in its maximum power mode without other workload. The latency covers the complete processing of a frame, from the image in memory to the final masks, and is the median over three passes after ten warm-up frames.\par}

{\small In a first experiment, NanoSAM~\cite{nanosam2023}, executed with TensorRT engines built on the device and a half-precision image encoder~\cite{nvidia_tensorrt}, was compared with the original SAM with a ViT-B encoder~\cite{kirillov2023segmentanything} at full precision in PyTorch. Both models were prompted with grids of $3 \times 3$, $5 \times 5$ and $7 \times 7$ points and post-processed identically: each prompt yields the single-mask output, a pixel belongs to the mask if its logit exceeds 0.80, masks below 3500 pixels are discarded, duplicates are removed by non-maximum suppression (NMS) at an IoU of 0.10, and the cap of 49 masks is never reached.\par}

{\small NanoSAM achieves the higher PQ at every grid density (Figure~\ref{fig:results_sam_backend}). The difference lies entirely in recognition quality, whereas the segmentation quality of ViT-B is equal or slightly higher: an object found by ViT-B is outlined at least as well. Both models keep a similar number of masks (10.4 and 10.7 per frame at $7 \times 7$), but both precision (0.384 against 0.478) and recall (0.360 against 0.437) are lower for ViT-B. With identical prompts, ViT-B thus more often returns a mask that covers an object only partly or extends beyond it, rather than more masks. Since the thresholds were chosen for NanoSAM, part of this gap may be due to the shared settings. NanoSAM is also 17 to 21 times faster; at $7 \times 7$, the ViT-B encoder alone takes about 370\,ms against 11\,ms, and the 49 decoder calls about 1300\,ms against 81\,ms. PQ grows with the grid density with diminishing returns, and even $7 \times 7$ recovers only 2286 of the 5231 objects, since many prompts fall on the same large surfaces and yield duplicate masks. These measurements confirm the choice of NanoSAM motivated in Section~\ref{sec:nanosam}.\par}

{\small A second experiment searched for a faster NanoSAM configuration. The input scale of 0.85, the cap of 8 masks and the minimum mask size of 3500 pixels were taken from the pipeline configuration, and three parameters were varied one at a time, each step retaining the values selected before: the grid density, the mask threshold (0.70, 0.80, 0.90) and the NMS IoU (0, 0.10, 0.15, 0.30). Within a step, the value with the highest PQ was selected, and among values within 0.005 of it the fastest; the grid density was chosen as a trade-off. Since the input scale and the mask cap differ from the first experiment, the two experiments are not directly comparable.\par}

{\small The grid density dominates the trade-off (Figure~\ref{fig:results_sam_grid}). PQ falls with sparser grids almost entirely through recall, while SQ (0.82 to 0.83) and precision (0.51 to 0.53) remain nearly constant: sparser prompting misses objects rather than producing poorer masks. The latency grows almost linearly with the number of prompts, since the encoder takes a constant 11\,ms. The loss is gradual down to $5 \times 5$ and steeper below; a $4 \times 4$ grid was selected as a balance between PQ and latency, with a 95th-percentile latency of only 44\,ms. The mask threshold changed PQ by at most 0.003 between 0.70 and 0.90, so 0.80 was kept. For the NMS, only the complete suppression of overlaps lowers PQ, since it discards about one valid mask per frame; between 0.10 and 0.30, PQ is unchanged, while the overlap between the kept masks triples. The selection rule picked 0.30 on a negligible latency difference, although 0.10 to 0.15 achieve the same PQ with less overlap between the resulting object crops. The resulting configuration, a $4 \times 4$ grid with a mask threshold of 0.80 and an NMS IoU of 0.30, reaches a PQ of 0.319 at 23.6 frames per second.\par}

{\small The pipeline itself uses a $3 \times 3$ grid instead of the selected $4 \times 4$; all other values agree with the study. Apart from the NMS IoU, which has no measurable effect in this range, this corresponds to the $3 \times 3$ point of Figure~\ref{fig:results_sam_grid_pps}: it gives up 0.026 in PQ for about 13\,ms less per frame, in order to leave more GPU time to Hydra and the vision-language model, with which segmentation shares the device (Section~\ref{sec:results_runtime}). The mask threshold of 0.80 is additionally supported by the tracking investigation, since a lower value enlarges the masks of touching objects and reduces the separation between their tracks. The consequences of the lower recall for tracking, in particular masks that merge touching surfaces, are examined in Section~\ref{sec:results_tracking}.\par}

{\small Three limitations apply to both experiments. Ground-truth objects are connected regions of one class, so that touching objects of the same class form a single object; all frames come from one recording and are not independent; and objects smaller than 3500 pixels are not evaluated. In addition, the parameter study selected and reports its values on the same frames, so that its PQ values are slightly optimistic.\par}
























\section{Object Tracking and Crop Selection}
\label{sec:results_tracking}

{\small This section evaluates the tracking of Section~\ref{sec:method_tracking}, which determines the geometry of each scene-graph node, and the view selection of Section~\ref{subsec:method_view_selection}, which determines the crop from which its label and risk attributes are derived. All tests replay the uHumans2 office sequence~\cite{rosinol2021kimera} at a rate of 0.1 on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin (Section~\ref{subsec:impl_phase1}) with ground-truth odometry.\par}

\subsection{Association Failure Modes and Tuning}
\label{subsec:results_assoc}

{\small The association (Section~\ref{subsec:method_association}) was first tuned to reduce duplicate tracks of objects seen from different viewpoints. The duplicates turned out to be scored in the recent mode rather than the revisit mode, so only adjusting the recent-mode threshold, weights and continuation window was effective; this reduced the number of tracks by about a quarter.\par}

{\small A review of the per-track crops and the logged cue scores then showed that some nodes contained two physical objects, caused by two distinct mechanisms. In \emph{segmentation-time fusion}, NanoSAM~\cite{nanosam2023} returns one mask covering two touching surfaces, which no association setting can undo (Section~\ref{sec:results_segmentation}).\par}

{\small In \emph{cross-track merges}, a clean track absorbs a separately segmented object, such as a floor absorbing a sofa. The analysis showed that the height and overlap votes pass for almost any object standing on a surface, so the quorum of two was met before the discriminating centroid and image cues could object.\par}

{\small The acceptance condition was therefore tightened step by step, mainly by raising the quorum to three, disabling the touch shortcut of the overlap vote and rejecting masks without usable depth. Unlike the first stage, this deliberately accepted more tracks, following the asymmetric-error principle of Section~\ref{sec:method_tracking}: a fragment can be merged later, whereas an over-merged node is wrong irreversibly.\par}

\subsection{Crop Selection}
\label{subsec:results_crop}

{\small For view selection, the earlier score was replaced by the additive score of Equation~\ref{eq:crop_score}, and the margin term was redefined to measure object pixels in a narrow edge zone rather than on the exact border, so that it distinguishes tightly cut views from well-framed ones. It was evaluated on the crop log of one run recorded without hysteresis.\par}

{\small The new score reduced the number of crop replacements per track (Figure~\ref{fig:results_crop}). The log also showed that, without hysteresis, many replacements exchanged nearly identical views, which motivated the relative improvement margin $h = 0.005$ of the deployed configuration. The scoring cost is negligible compared with model inference.\par}
