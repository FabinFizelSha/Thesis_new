\section{Segmentation Backend and Configuration}
\label{sec:results_segmentation}

{\small The segmentation models for object proposals (Section~\ref{subsec:method_proposals}) were evaluated offline on stored frames, outside the running pipeline, so that their masks could be compared with ground truth and their latency measured in isolation. The benchmark consists of 500 frames of the uHumans2 office scene~\cite{rosinol2021kimera} at $720 \times 480$ pixels, sampled about every 0.75\,s. The simulator provides a segmentation image that labels each pixel with a class but does not distinguish objects of the same class. Each connected region of one class was therefore taken as one ground-truth object; as a consequence, touching objects of the same class, such as the two water dispensers in Figure~\ref{fig:results_gt_example}, form a single object. Regions smaller than 3500 pixels were ignored in the evaluation. This gives 5231 objects, on average 10.5 per frame (Figure~\ref{fig:results_gt_example}).\par}

{\small The masks were scored with the panoptic quality (PQ) of Kirillov et~al.~\cite{kirillov2019panoptic}, applied without class labels. A mask $p$ and a ground-truth object $g$ match if their intersection over union (IoU) exceeds 0.5; since neither masks nor objects overlap, each object has at most one such mask, and the result is independent of the order of the masks. Unmatched objects count as false negatives ($\mathit{FN}$), unmatched masks as false positives ($\mathit{FP}$). Pooled over all frames,
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{PQ} =
	\underbrace{\frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \mathit{TP}} \mathrm{IoU}(p,g)}{|\mathit{TP}|}}_{\text{segmentation quality (SQ)}}
	\times
	\underbrace{\frac{|\mathit{TP}|}{|\mathit{TP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathit{FP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathit{FN}|}}_{\text{recognition quality (RQ)}} ,
	\label{eq:pq}
\end{equation}
where $\mathit{TP}$ is the set of matched pairs. SQ measures how closely the found objects are outlined, RQ, an F1 score, how many objects are found without spurious masks. All runs were performed on a Jetson AGX Thor (JetPack~7.2, TensorRT~10.16) in its maximum power mode without other workload. The latency covers the complete processing of a frame, from the image in memory to the final masks, and is the median over three passes after ten warm-up frames.\par}

{\small In a first experiment, NanoSAM~\cite{nanosam2023} was compared with SAM with a ViT-B encoder~\cite{kirillov2023segmentanything} (PyTorch, full precision), both prompted with $3 \times 3$, $5 \times 5$ and $7 \times 7$ grids and post-processed identically: single-mask output per prompt, logit threshold 0.80, minimum mask size 3500 pixels and non-maximum suppression (NMS) at an IoU of 0.10. At every grid density, NanoSAM is more accurate and about ten times faster than ViT-B (Figure~\ref{fig:results_sam_backend}). The difference comes from how many objects are found, not from how well they are outlined. Both models produce about the same number of masks per frame, and the objects that ViT-B does find are outlined just as well. However, fewer of its masks correspond to a real object: they more often cover only part of an object or spill over onto its neighbours. Denser grids help both models with diminishing returns, as many prompts fall on the same large surfaces, and even the densest grid finds fewer than half of the objects. These results confirm the choice of NanoSAM (Section~\ref{sec:nanosam}).\par}

{\small A second experiment aimed to find a NanoSAM configuration that is fast enough for the pipeline while keeping as much of the segmentation quality as possible, and to check whether the overlap between the generated masks can be reduced without losing accuracy. It used the same frames, scoring and timing as the first experiment, but took the input scale of 0.85, the cap of 8 masks and the minimum mask size of 3500 pixels from the pipeline configuration; the two experiments are therefore not directly comparable. Three parameters were varied one at a time, each step keeping the values chosen before. The grid density was varied from $7 \times 7$ to $3 \times 3$ and chosen as a trade-off between PQ and latency. The mask threshold (0.70, 0.80, 0.90) was then tested for its effect on PQ. Finally, the NMS IoU (0, 0.10, 0.15, 0.30) was varied to determine how strongly overlapping masks can be suppressed without reducing PQ, since overlapping masks lead to overlapping object crops.\par}

{\small Sparser grids lose PQ almost entirely through recall (Figure~\ref{fig:results_sam_grid}), while SQ and precision stay nearly constant: fewer prompts miss objects rather than producing poorer masks. Since the encoder takes a constant 11\,ms, the latency is governed by the number of prompts. The loss of PQ steepens below $5 \times 5$, and a $4 \times 4$ grid was chosen as the balance, with a 95th-percentile latency of only 44\,ms. The mask threshold has almost no effect on PQ, so a conservative value of 0.80 was chosen. For the NMS, only the complete suppression of overlaps costs accuracy, because it removes about one valid mask per frame; any IoU from 0.10 upwards gives the same PQ, and 0.30 was chosen, since it keeps slightly more masks per frame while the overlap remains a few per cent of the pixels.\par}

{\small The pipeline itself uses a $3 \times 3$ grid instead of the selected $4 \times 4$; all other values agree with the study. Apart from the NMS IoU, which has no measurable effect in this range, this corresponds to the $3 \times 3$ point of Figure~\ref{fig:results_sam_grid_pps}: it gives up 0.026 in PQ for about 13\,ms less per frame, in order to leave more GPU time to Hydra and the vision-language model, with which segmentation shares the device (Section~\ref{sec:results_runtime}). The mask threshold of 0.80 is additionally supported by the tracking investigation, since a lower value enlarges the masks of touching objects and reduces the separation between their tracks. The consequences of the lower recall for tracking, in particular masks that merge touching surfaces, are examined in Section~\ref{sec:results_tracking}.\par}

\section{Object Tracking and Crop Selection}
\label{sec:results_tracking}

{\small Tracking and view selection (Sections~\ref{sec:method_tracking} and~\ref{subsec:method_view_selection}) were evaluated online, in the running pipeline, since both depend on the sequence of observations rather than on individual frames. All experiments replay the office sequence of uHumans2~\cite{rosinol2021kimera} with ground-truth odometry at a rate of 0.1 (about 1.6\,fps camera input) on a Jetson AGX Orin, using the implementation of Section~\ref{subsec:impl_phase1}. Because the odometry is exact, the map and odometry frames coincide and loop-closure re-anchoring is never triggered, so that the results reflect the association alone. Two records were used throughout: a log with one entry per mask-to-track decision, containing the association mode and the individual cue scores, and crops of each track saved at regular intervals, which show the raw single-frame mask. Where runs of different length are compared, the number of tracks is normalised per 100 processed frames.\par}

{\small To evaluate the quality of the tracking, an inspection setup was first established: after a run on a 300\,s segment, the stored crops of all tracks were arranged in a gallery, one row per track, so that the tracks could be compared visually. For the initial configuration (A1), this inspection revealed duplicate tracks, that is, several tracks for one object seen from different viewpoints. A first experiment therefore tuned the parameters of the association score (Equation~\ref{eq:association_score}, Section~\ref{subsec:method_association}) to remove them. Since every run replays the same segment, the total number of tracks served as the measure. The parameters were changed in five steps, A2 to A6; after each step, the number of tracks was recorded, the association log showed in which mode, recent or revisit, the duplicates had been evaluated, and the gallery was inspected again. The first two steps addressed the revisit mode: lowering its threshold $s_{\min}$ from 0.75 to 0.40 (A2) and raising its image weight to 0.35 (A3) raised the number of tracks from 86 to 93 and 92. The log showed why: the duplicates were created about 0.5\,s after the last update of the original track, within the continuation window of 15\,s, and were therefore evaluated in the recent mode. The remaining steps addressed this mode. Lowering its threshold to 0.40 and raising its image weight from 0.25 to 0.35 (A4) gave 79 tracks; lowering the threshold to 0.30 and shortening the continuation window to 8\,s (A5) gave 64, 26\,\% below the initial value; and rebalancing the weights towards the volumetric overlap (A6) gave 63. The recent-mode weights $w_k$ finally adopted are 0.70 for the volumetric overlap, 0.30 for the centroid and 0.45 for the image overlap.\par}

{\small The number of tracks, however, does not guarantee that each track contains only one object. A second experiment therefore inspected the content of the tracks, with the aim of finding nodes that combine two physical objects and removing their cause. For every track, the crop of every tenth observation was saved with the contour of that frame's own mask, together with the 3D box of the observation and the accumulated box of the track, and inspected manually. If the mask of a single frame already outlined two objects, the error came from the segmentation: in this \emph{mask fusion}, anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:results_segmentation}, NanoSAM returns one mask covering two touching surfaces, which the tracker cannot separate. If every mask was correct but the box of the track had grown around a second object, the association had added the mask of another object to the track; the frame at which this happened was located in the decision log by a sudden jump of the object position and an abrupt change of the mask size. Each inspected observation was manually marked as a wrong merge or a correct continuation. The log showed why such \emph{cross-track merges} occurred: two of the five tests of Section~\ref{subsec:method_association} sufficed for a match, and two of them, height compatibility and box overlap, are passed by almost any object standing on a large surface, so the centroid distance and the image overlap, the only tests that distinguish a surface from an object resting on it, had no influence. Since changing the weights did not prevent this, the acceptance rule was made stricter in successive runs, R1 to R6, each building on the previous one (Figure~\ref{fig:results_tracking_campaign}): R2 required three agreeing tests and a stricter centroid limit, R3 repeated this setting, R4 raised the required box overlap, R5 disabled a shortcut that counted touching boxes as overlapping, and R6 discarded masks without enough valid depth for a 3D box. Each change was kept only if, replayed on the logged decisions, it rejected all marked wrong merges and accepted all marked correct continuations. After R6, the crops showed none of the confirmed merges, and its configuration was adopted as final. Following the asymmetric-error principle of Section~\ref{sec:method_tracking}, the resulting fragmentation was accepted: it rose from 3.2 to at most 11.1 tracks per 100 frames and settled at 9.4.\par}

{\small A third experiment measured how reliably objects are re-identified when the pipeline is restarted in an environment it has already mapped. The same 300\,s segment, corresponding to 147 to 148 processed frames, was replayed in five consecutive sessions with the final association configuration. Session persistence (Section~\ref{subsec:method_sessions}) was enabled for this experiment only, and the memory was cleared only before the first session. Each later session resumed from the state saved by the previous one, with the stored timestamps shifted so that restored tracks were first evaluated in the revisit mode. A re-encountered object counts as re-identified if it is associated with a stored track in the revisit mode instead of opening a new track. As Figure~\ref{fig:results_revisit} shows, the first session created all tracks, whereas the following sessions created almost none and associated nearly every re-encountered object as a revisit. In total, 97.5\,\% of the re-encountered objects were re-identified; three of the five misses lie close together above 3.3\,m and probably belong to one tall structure whose centroid shifts beyond the centroid limit as the viewpoint changes. Since restored tracks keep their labels, the number of labelling calls dropped to nearly zero, while the throughput remained unchanged across sessions (Section~\ref{sec:results_runtime}).\par}

{\small To evaluate the view selection of Section~\ref{subsec:method_view_selection}, which chooses the crop passed to the vision-language model for each track, the crops selected during a run were logged together with the individual terms of their score. A fourth experiment used this log to tune the additive score of Equation~\ref{eq:crop_score}, a weighted sum of pixel count, sharpness, measured as the variance of the Laplacian~\cite{pechpacheco2000laplacian}, and margin. The margin term was first redefined to count object pixels in a 3\,px edge zone instead of on the exact crop border, which spread its values from 0.943--0.991 to 0.476--0.967 and thus made it able to separate tightly cut views from well-framed ones. The weights were then adjusted qualitatively on the crop log of one run with 16 tracks. Since the log contains the three terms of every selected crop, each candidate weighting could be replayed offline, and the replacements it would have accepted were inspected visually. Six ratios $w_P : w_S : w_M$ were compared, namely 1:1:1, 3:1:1, 4:1:1, 5:1:1, 2:2:1 and 2:2:0.5, using two criteria: a replacement that clearly improves the view must still be accepted, such as a gain of 0.59\,\% in Track~1 caused by better framing, whereas replacements between nearly identical views from consecutive frames should be avoided. Equal weights met the first criterion but not the second, as they still accepted many replacements between nearly identical views. Ratios dominated by the pixel term (3:1:1 to 5:1:1) met the second but not the first: they penalised small objects strongly and rejected genuine improvements, including the one in Track~1. The ratios 2:2:1 and 2:2:0.5 met both criteria and selected almost identical crops. The ratio 2:2:1, that is, $w_P = w_S = 0.4$ and $w_M = 0.2$, was adopted. It gives pixel count and sharpness equal and dominant weight, since both determine how much the vision-language model can see, while the margin term acts as a secondary criterion that favours better framing without rejecting a crop that is large and sharp but tightly cut; of the two equivalent ratios, the round value 1 for the margin was kept for clarity. The score takes about 1\,ms per crop.\par}

{\small The pipeline uses the final association configuration of the second experiment (quorum of three, centroid gate 0.60\,m, overlap threshold 0.50, containment threshold 0.92, touch shortcut disabled) and the crop score with $h = 0.005$. Session persistence is disabled by default, so that every run in the following sections starts from an empty map. The selected crops form the input of the labelling evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:results_vlm}; tracks affected by mask fusion receive a crop that shows two objects.\par}

\section{VLM Model and Prompt Comparison}
\label{sec:results_vlm}

{\small The vision-language model and its prompt were evaluated online, in the running pipeline, so that the model received exactly the crops produced by the tracking and view selection of Section~\ref{sec:results_tracking}. Every run replayed the office sequence of uHumans2~\cite{rosinol2021kimera} at a rate of 0.1 on a Jetson AGX Orin and was stopped after 700\,s of wall-clock time, about 70\,s of scene time, so that all runs covered the same part of the scene and produced about 50 object crops each. Retrieval was disabled and its memory cleared before every run, so that every unlabelled object was sent to the vision-language model. All other settings were those of the production configuration; only the model and the prompt were changed. The benchmark concerns the identification of objects only: the prompts compared request the label, the mobility class and their confidences, but no risk-relevant information, so the visual-state description of Section~\ref{subsec:method_vlm_contract} was not part of it.\par}

{\small For every labelling call, the pipeline saved the crop as sent to the model, together with the returned label, mobility class and confidences, the validation result and the inference time reported by llama.cpp~\cite{llamacpp}. After each run, the crops were inspected manually, the correct label was recorded and every wrong answer was assigned to an error type. Accuracy is strict: an answer counts as correct only if both label and mobility class are right, although, since all objects in this scene are static, mobility never cost a point.\par}

{\small A first experiment used Qwen3-VL-8B at full precision (FP16)~\cite{bai2025qwen3vl}, the model of the baseline pipeline. Three prompts of increasing complexity, P1 to P3, were compared; they are given in Appendix~\ref{app:vlm_prompts}. P2 and P3 were each revised once against the errors of the preceding prompt on this model and then fixed. As the Qwen3 bars in Figure~\ref{fig:results_vlm_acc} show, P2 raised the accuracy from 48 to 72\,\%, whereas P3 fell back to 63\,\%. The rules and examples of P2 removed the main errors of P1, in which the model named an object outside the contour or a part instead of the whole object, whereas the additional guidance of P3 introduced new confusions between similar classes rather than resolving the remaining errors. P1 also yielded a nearly constant confidence on every crop, wrong answers included, so the validation gate never fired; only P3 produced values below 0.80, and both answers rejected for this reason were correct. At 4.8 to 6.3\,s per call, the model was also slow.\par}

{\small A second experiment repeated the unchanged prompts with Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen2026qwen35}, both quantised to 4 bits (Q4\_K\_M) and run with reasoning disabled, since the thinking block otherwise consumed the token budget. As the Qwen3.5 bars in Figure~\ref{fig:results_vlm} show, the accuracy of the 4B model rose with every more detailed prompt, from 64\,\% with P1 to 88\,\% with P3, whereas the 9B model reached only 64 to 68\,\% with all three prompts. The larger model was thus not more accurate. On both Qwen3.5 models, P2 removed the main errors of P1 and P3 removed most of the remaining ones. The 9B, however, kept confusing visually similar classes whatever the prompt, which indicates a limitation of the model itself. As with the 8B, only P3 led the models to report confidences below the threshold of 0.80. One problem occurred only on the smaller models: with the first version of P3, the 4B wrote out its step-by-step reasoning in the reply and used up the token budget before giving the JSON answer. A rule at the beginning of the prompt stating that the reply must consist of exactly one JSON object, illustrated by a good and a bad example, solved this, and all answers were then valid.\par}

{\small The 4B model with the format-hardened P3 reached 88\,\% at a median of 2.9\,s and is the most accurate, fastest and least memory-intensive pair. The pipeline therefore uses the profile \texttt{qwen3\_5\_4b\_q4} with reasoning disabled, a temperature of 0, 512 tokens and P3 as the basis of its prompt; the minimum label confidence was kept at 0.80, so that uncertain labels are retried with a better crop. Since Qwen3.5-4B was at least as accurate as the baseline Qwen3-VL-8B with every prompt and 1.6 to 2.3 times faster, and was adopted by changing only the model profile and the prompt, the comparison supports the expectation of Section~\ref{subsec:qwen} that newer model generations improve the pipeline without changes to its other components. Its latency in the complete pipeline is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results_runtime}.\par}

{\small The risk-relevant information was built on top of the prompt selected in this comparison. For the risk assessment, the deployed prompt asks the model, in addition to the label and the mobility class, for the visual-state description of Section~\ref{subsec:method_vlm_contract}, a short phrase stating what is visibly particular about the object at the time of observation, for instance items placed on a table or whether a monitor is switched on. The description was added as a separate section appended to P3, so that the selected prompt itself remains unchanged and only the output contract is extended by a fifth field; the complete active prompt is given in Appendix~\ref{app:vlm_prompt_deployed}. Since this extension was introduced after the comparison, neither its effect on the label accuracy reported above nor the quality of the descriptions has been measured.\par}

\section{Runtime Performance}
\label{sec:results_runtime}

{\small This section quantifies the throughput, per-frame latency and resource demand of the pipeline described in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} on the two embedded platforms. In all runs, the uHumans2 office bag~\cite{rosinol2021kimera} (\texttt{office\_s1\_00h\_v2}, 506\,s, RGB at 16.41\,Hz) was replayed at a rate of 1.0, so that the input exceeds the processing capacity of either platform, frames are dropped and the maximum sustained throughput is measured. The Jetson AGX Thor~\cite{nvidia_jetson_thor} (power mode MAXN) was evaluated in three runs of 300\,s, each starting from an empty memory, in the final configuration (object VLM Qwen3.5-4B~\cite{qwen2026qwen35}, Q4\_K\_M, prompt P3); the first 20 frames of each run are excluded as warm-up. The Jetson AGX Orin~\cite{nvidia_jetson_orin} (power mode MODE\_50W) serves as the reference. Timings come from the per-frame trace of the pipeline, resource figures from \texttt{tegrastats} and \texttt{nvidia-smi}.\par}

{\small Only the throughput figures are strictly like-for-like, since both platforms ran the same final configuration. Thor processes about twice as many frames per second as Orin (factor 2.03, Figure~\ref{fig:results_runtime_throughput}a), that is, about 30\,\% of the input frames instead of 14\,\%; the variation across the three Thor runs is small, all dropped frames were discarded by the drop-oldest policy of the input queue, and no frame failed. The per-frame Phase~1 latency, measured from frame arrival until publication to Hydra (Section~\ref{subsec:impl_phase1}), is reduced by a factor of about 2.6 on average (Figure~\ref{fig:results_runtime_throughput}b).\par}

{\small The pipeline is GPU-bound on Thor. During processing, the GPU was busy about 90\,\% of the time on average, whereas the CPU load stayed at about 40\,\%. Memory use remained at about 24\,GiB, a fraction of the 122\,GiB available. The board drew about 81\,W on average and at most 109\,W, which corresponds to about 17\,J per processed frame, and the GPU temperature did not exceed 63\,$^{\circ}$C. Downstream of Phase~1, the update rates were measured on the published topics, so they are output rates rather than internal computation times. Hydra~\cite{hughes2022hydra} (Section~\ref{subsec:impl_hydra}) published a new scene graph at about 1.4\,Hz, and a processed frame typically appeared in the next graph within about 0.4\,s (median). The Fuser updated its output at only about 0.3\,Hz instead of the configured 1\,Hz. The risk layer thus receives a refreshed scene graph at about 1.4\,Hz, but a fused, risk-annotated view only about every 3 to 4\,s. Overall, the Jetson AGX Thor processes about 4.8 frames per second, roughly twice the rate of the Orin, and geometry and object tracking keep pace with this rate, so that each processed frame reaches Hydra within about 0.3\,s on average. Semantic labelling, in contrast, lags considerably on a first visit and catches up only once objects are revisited, which defines the principal runtime limitation discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}.\par}
